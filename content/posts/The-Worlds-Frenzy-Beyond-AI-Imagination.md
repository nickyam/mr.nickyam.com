Last night, across the Pacific, came word that Jensen Huang — forced out as Nvidia's CEO under board pressure — had been unanimously voted back in. Nvidia's stock reversed and ripped upward, US equities surged, and crypto followed with a sharp rebound. BTC ran from just above $60k to nearly $65k intraday, up more than 5% at the session's high [1].

One modest green candle was enough to make people who, three days earlier, had panicked when BTC wicked down to $55k and called for $50k or even $40k, start regretting their empty positions — regretting not having bought the fear. (No need to regret: by morning all the gains had been given back. Just try not to forget that earlier regret every time the market reverses to pick people up.)

Someone summed it up like this: "The company fires me, global assets crash, a financial crisis hits, the US president and the world's richest man jointly beg me to return — and on the day I come back, global assets surge." Not even the most wildly imagined web novel would dare write that plot. Yet it happened, vividly and for real.

I remembered an old saying: reality is stranger than fiction. Why? Because fiction is made up by people, and human-made plots carry logic, overt or covert — while the real world can be absurd on a scale that ignores logic altogether.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent demonstrated exactly this kind of "logic." On a television interview, the host asked: you previously said Nvidia should find a suitable outsider to run it, from a national-security standpoint — so what do you make of Jensen Huang's return? Guess what Bessent said. Yes, he had said Nvidia should find an outsider for the CEO role — but Huang, having resigned, was now an outsider himself, and so on and so forth [2].

Using logical consistency to reason and predict is the strength of intelligence — it helps one adapt better to a changing world. It is also intelligence's weakness: sometimes the world is wildly, madly illogical.

The world's frenzy often exceeds even the widest flights of human imagination, let alone the imagination of AI, which in this respect falls short of the human brain.

Thinking about this, I came to see why you cannot place your investment judgment in the hands of a large language model — even as AI improves day by day, even as it begins to surpass humans at the very highest intellectual challenges, such as proving the hardest mathematical problems.

## Engineering problems, where AI excels

Whether it is AlphaGo mastering Go, or Claude or GPT proving hard math theorems, these breakthroughs come from breaking through the brain's computational bottleneck. But the problems these AIs solve are logical — strictly logical, without a trace of illogic.

Because A, therefore B. Because B, therefore C. Link upon link, seamless, airtight. This is exactly where AI excels.

If I drive at 100 km/h on the highway to a city 100 km away, leaving at eight, I can predict that — absent accidents — I will arrive a little after nine, in time for a 9:30 meeting. That predictive power comes from the physics formula: speed equals distance divided by time. Physics formulas are logical.

But if in 2026 I am executing a bear-market bottom-fishing plan, and by November 21 I am 90% invested, having endured an extreme wick down to $55.1k — then I simply cannot predict whether I should go all in immediately to meet a bull-market recovery, or keep buying cautiously and wait for the final bottom. The secondary market does not run on logic.

## If not logic, then what? Interest

If the secondary market does not run on logic, what does it run on? Interest. In the adult world, there is only interest.

The illogical candlestick chart swings up and down, rain at the turn of a hand. Self-media accounts and analysts that sway with the wind are either fools, pushed around by the market, or accomplices of the operators, helping release news to shape expectations. The interests of the majority of retail investors are harvested again and again, while a minority of winners pocket their gains hand over fist.

Faced with an illogical market, anyone who analyzes it with logic is either a fool, if sincere, or an accomplice, if posturing.

Logical analysis has exactly two kinds of input material: first, everything that has happened in the market, now and in the past, including prices; second, the logical analyses of the market produced by the two types above — the genuinely foolish and the play-acting foolish — all the opinions, articles, and reports.

What is logical analysis? Any logical proposition you utter is logical analysis. The most common form is attribution: because such-and-such, therefore so-and-so. The moment you say "because... therefore," you are speaking logically.

Why will people insist on logic to the death? Because without logic, nobody believes you — and even fabricated logic can fool a lot of people. If someone says outright that BTC is destined for $1 million, you will dismiss them as a shill. But if a research report says that, because of such-and-such market segmentation, statistical fitting, and valuation models, BTC will hit these targets by such-and-such a year — an optimistic $1 million, a base case of $500k, a pessimistic $300k — you cannot help but half believe it.

But markets have an iron law: the few make money, the many lose.

Losing has nothing to do with ability, cognition, or luck, and nothing to do with any explanation — the moment you try to explain, you are back to logic. You lose simply because you have become part of the crowd, and so losing is inevitable.

Most people cannot overcome the instinct to force logic onto an illogical market, and so the logic-speaking majority lose, broadly.

## The model learned the crowd

Today's large language models are trained precisely in the image of that vast majority.

The data used to train them is exactly those two kinds described above. Of those two kinds, the first is past facts, which say nothing about any future state; the second is subjective human expression, and beneath the veil of logic, it is nothing but human weakness.

We used to think that, not being human, AI would rise above human baseness and reach a state of rational, objective purity.

But when I actually faced real markets and the real world alongside a large language model, I was startled to find that it had absorbed the purest form of human weakness.

Think it through: a large language model learns from vast quantities of human data and becomes the average of the majority — what we call the "consensus" of human knowledge.

And unfortunately, as noted above, the market's iron law is this: the closer you are to the average of the majority, the more you resemble the crowd, the closer you are to losing.

Bullish when it rises, bearish when it falls. Extrapolating the future from historical data, hunting for logical explanations for market swings — this is the most common human weakness of ordinary people steeped in the market, and the model has learned it verbatim.

## Engineering, speculation, gambling

I cannot help but recall the economist Mises's *Human Action*, which I read years ago. In it, Mises divides human action into three kinds: engineering, speculation, and gambling [3].

Go, mathematics, and physics — the logical disciplines — are engineering problems. Humans can grasp the rules, control the conditions, and accurately predict what a given action will produce. These are things a large language model can help with, and even replace humans in.

Illogical market investing, business management, and political maneuvering are all speculation. The English word "speculate" lacks the derogatory tone carried by the Chinese term; it means betting on any future uncertainty. Humans can never fully eliminate risk; they must judge uncertainty and place a bet — make a decision. In these matters, a large language model will most likely do more harm than good.

The part that is speculation must be left to humans.

There is an investment maxim: buy when the market is bleeding, even if the blood is your own.

What lets a person buy boldly at the moment of maximum panic, what gives them the courage to act through their own bleeding, is not logic, not analysis — it is conviction.

A price drop is an opportunity only if the price is higher later — and whether it will be higher later, no historical data or logical analysis can prove. That is precisely what the efficient market hypothesis says: all public information is already in the price; the rest, nobody can foresee [4].

The illogic of the secondary market is beyond a large language model's comprehension. The frenzy of the real world goes beyond everything it can imagine.
