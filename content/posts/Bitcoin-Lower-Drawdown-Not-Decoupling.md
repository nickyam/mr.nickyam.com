This morning Bitcoin kept oscillating near its 50-day moving average, around $81.8k. That line, since about October 8–9, has become the support Bitcoin keeps pulling back to and leaning on. As of today, October 23, it still has not been broken.

Since October 2, Bitcoin is down roughly 3%. Over the same window, the Nasdaq 100 fell about 4% and the semiconductor index (SOX) dropped 14% [1]. Look back one more round: from June 22 to July 29, Bitcoin was essentially flat while the Nasdaq fell 10% and SOX fell 29% [1].

Across those two rounds, Coinbase Institutional's conclusion is that the market no longer treats Bitcoin as a leveraged proxy for tech stocks.

That conclusion has its merits. In past years, whenever risk assets sold off, Bitcoin was often dumped first as a high-beta instrument, because its holder base was loaded with leveraged capital. After that leverage cleared out, the remaining holders skewed toward long-term allocators, so Bitcoin's sensitivity to tech-stock volatility naturally came down. With two rounds of data on the table, the direction is at least correct.

But two things still need to be kept separate: falling less, and decoupling, may not be the same thing.

Falling less could simply mean beta has dropped. Beta is an asset's sensitivity to a benchmark, and it declines for two distinct reasons. One is that correlation is genuinely severed — Bitcoin starts following its own logic. The other is that Bitcoin's own volatility has been flattened — it has just gone quiet, while the benchmark keeps moving as before. Today's market likely contains both. Glassnode notes Bitcoin is pinned in a $80k–$87k box, with its daily Bollinger Bands squeezing to their tightest in two and a half years and volatility crushed to extreme lows [2]. An asset whose volatility has been compressed simply *looks* resilient on the way down.

Decoupling is a different matter. It means the pricing anchor has switched — Bitcoin no longer dances to Nasdaq liquidity but instead follows its own supply and demand. For that claim to hold, two things must happen together: first, when risk assets crash, Bitcoin not only fails to fall but actually rises; second, when Bitcoin rises, it is not because tech stocks are rising. Neither condition has appeared yet. In this October round, Bitcoin fell 3% against the Nasdaq's 4% — a gap of just one percentage point. Calling that decoupling is far too early.

There is also a piece of counter-evidence that the Coinbase report did not mention. This week, stablecoin reserves on Binance fell to their lowest level of the year, down about 4.7% over 90 days; on October 20, whales moved 10,605 BTC onto exchanges in a single day — 2.8 times the 30-day daily average [3]. Liquidity is tightening and chips are flowing toward exchanges. The fact that Bitcoin has not crashed under these conditions may be because the selling pressure has not yet been released, not because it has grown stronger.

So where is Bitcoin's pricing anchor actually drifting?

One clue is worth watching. Since the second half of October, Bitcoin's returns have been driven mainly by the Asian session, with Europe dragging: as of October 20, the Asian session was cumulatively +3.9%, the US session +1.1%, and the European session −5.5%, while a buy-and-hold-through-the-day return was −0.7% [4]. The same asset, three time zones, three different outcomes — a sign that the participant structure is fragmented. Asia is buying, Europe is selling, and the US is ambivalent.

If this pattern persists, marginal pricing power will gradually shift from Western institutions to Asian capital. The transfer of pricing power tends to come bundled with a reconstruction of correlations, because the new marginal buyer carries its own set of constraints and judgments. That explains more about what is happening than simply staring at the Bitcoin–Nasdaq correlation coefficient.

The more balanced read is that Coinbase's direction is worth taking seriously, but its conclusion arrives early. A lower beta is a fact; decoupling is an inference; between the two sits a stretch of time that still needs verifying. What would truly prove decoupling is not Bitcoin falling a few points less when tech sells off, but Bitcoin tracing its own path when tech sells off.

For now, that independent move is not yet visible. The trading box still holds, liquidity is still draining, and the catalyst has not arrived. The real question is this: when Bitcoin stops following tech to the downside, how does it surge? The answer may lie not in liquidity, but in the rules.
